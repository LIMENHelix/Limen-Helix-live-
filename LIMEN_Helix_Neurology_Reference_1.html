LIMEN Helix — Comprehensive Neurology & Nervous System Reference

Purpose. A structured, exhaustive map of the human nervous system and everything that couples to it — neurons, glia, signaling, neurotransmission, neuroanatomy, all innervations, every sensory system and its outside sources, the organs and endocrine/immune systems the nervous system controls, the circuit dynamics that make it compute, and the failure modes at each level. Scope is restricted, per request, to what participates directly or indirectly in nervous-system dynamics.

How to read this. Definitions are given in-line the first time a term appears. The document moves from parts (cells) → wiring (anatomy, innervation) → inputs (senses/organs) → dynamics (how the circuits actually compute) → pathology (how they break). The final section is a consolidated parameter table intended as the quantitative substrate for the LIMEN Helix build.

Confidence legend. Every non-trivial claim is flagged: (high) = replicated, textbook consensus; (medium) = well-supported but with active debate on details; (low) = suggestive evidence, not settled; (speculative) = plausible, not established.

Citation policy. Load-bearing dynamics and quantitative/timing claims are anchored to peer-reviewed sources with verified DOIs (retrieved and confirmed via PubMed; see References). Broad anatomical and definitional content is standard consensus neuroscience (Kandel Principles of Neural Science; Purves Neuroscience; Gray's Anatomy) and is flagged (high, consensus) rather than pinned to a single DOI — this is stated honestly rather than attaching invented citations. No DOI in this document is AI-generated; every DOI listed was returned by PubMed.

PART I — ORGANIZATION OF THE NERVOUS SYSTEM

I.1 The two anatomical divisions

  • Central nervous system (CNS) — brain + spinal cord. The integrative core: it receives, stores, transforms, and commands. (high, consensus)
  • Peripheral nervous system (PNS) — all neural tissue outside the CNS: cranial nerves (except CN II, which is a CNS tract), spinal nerves, ganglia, sensory receptors, and enteric neurons. The interface: it carries information in (afferent) and commands out (efferent). (high, consensus)

I.2 The functional divisions

  • Somatic nervous system — voluntary; innervates skeletal muscle (efferent) and carries conscious somatosensation (afferent). (high)
  • Autonomic nervous system (ANS) — involuntary control of viscera, glands, smooth muscle, cardiac muscle. Subdivides into sympathetic (mobilization / "fight-or-flight"), parasympathetic ("rest-and-digest" / restorative), and is often grouped with the enteric system. (high) Detailed in Part VIII.
  • Enteric nervous system (ENS) — the gut's semi-autonomous network (~200–600 million neurons), capable of local reflexes without CNS input; the "second brain." (high) Detailed in Part IX.

I.3 Core structural vocabulary (definitions)

  • Gray matter — regions dense in neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, synapses (cortex, nuclei). Where computation/integration happens.
  • White matter — myelinated axon tracts; the wiring that connects gray-matter regions. Appears white because of myelin lipid.
  • Nucleus (CNS) — a cluster of neuron cell bodies inside the CNS with a shared function (e.g., red nucleus).
  • Ganglion (PNS) — a cluster of neuron cell bodies outside the CNS (e.g., dorsal root ganglion).
  • Tract / pathway (CNS) vs nerve (PNS) — a bundle of axons; "tract" inside the CNS, "nerve" outside.
  • Afferent = toward CNS (sensory in). Efferent = away from CNS (motor/command out).
  • Decussation — crossing of a tract to the opposite (contralateral) side; the reason one hemisphere controls the opposite body half.

I.4 Protection, fluid, and clearance (indirect but load-bearing)

  • Meninges — three protective layers: dura mater (tough outer), arachnoid mater (middle), pia mater (delicate inner). Subarachnoid space carries CSF and vessels. (high)
  • Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) — produced by the choroid plexus in the ventricles; cushions, buoys, and chemically stabilizes the CNS; circulates through the ventricular system to the subarachnoid space. (high)
  • Ventricular system — four connected cavities (two lateral, third, fourth ventricles) containing CSF.
  • Blood–brain barrier (BBB) — tight-junctioned endothelium + astrocyte end-feet + pericytes that selectively gate what enters the CNS from blood. A chemical firewall. (high) ⟨fractal-critical: selective gating at a boundary⟩
  • Glymphatic system — a glia-(astrocyte/AQP4)-dependent perivascular clearance pathway that flushes soluble waste (including amyloid-β) from brain interstitium, markedly enhanced during sleep. According to PubMed, this waste-clearance function and its coupling to sleep/aging are reviewed by Benveniste et al., 2018 (DOI). (medium — mechanism established in rodents, human details still active) ⟨fractal-critical: offline maintenance / homeostatic clearance⟩

PART II — CELLS OF THE NERVOUS SYSTEM

II.1 The neuron (definition + parts)

A neuron is an electrically excitable cell that receives, integrates, and transmits information via electrochemical signals. Parts: - Dendrites — branched input surface; receive synaptic contacts; site of local integration and (in many cells) active dendritic computation. - Soma (cell body) — houses nucleus and metabolic machinery; integrates inputs toward the axon hillock. - Axon hillock / initial segment — the trigger zone where the action potential is initiated (highest Na⁺-channel density). - Axon — the output cable; can be <1 mm to >1 m long; may be myelinated. - Myelin sheath — insulating wraps (oligodendrocytes in CNS, Schwann cells in PNS) that speed conduction. - Nodes of Ranvier — gaps between myelin segments where the action potential regenerates (saltatory conduction). - Axon terminals / boutons — output sites releasing neurotransmitter onto targets.

II.2 Neuron classifications

By function: sensory (afferent) neurons carry input from receptors; motor (efferent) neurons drive muscles/glands; interneurons (the vast majority in CNS) connect neurons within circuits, doing local computation. (high)

By polarity/morphology: multipolar (most CNS neurons), bipolar (retina, olfactory), pseudounipolar (dorsal root ganglion sensory), unipolar. (high)

By projection scale: projection neurons (long-range, typically glutamatergic/excitatory, e.g., cortical pyramidal cells) vs local interneurons (typically GABAergic/inhibitory). ⟨fractal-critical: long-range excitation + local inhibition is the canonical motif⟩

By neurotransmitter: glutamatergic (excitatory), GABAergic/glycinergic (inhibitory), cholinergic, dopaminergic, serotonergic, noradrenergic, histaminergic, peptidergic (see Part IV).

Key cortical interneuron classes (⟨fractal-critical⟩): PV⁺ (parvalbumin, fast-spiking, perisomatic inhibition → controls spike timing/gain), SOM⁺ (somatostatin, dendrite-targeting → controls input integration), VIP⁺ (vasoactive intestinal peptide, disinhibitory — inhibits other interneurons). The interplay of perisomatic (PV⁺) vs dendritic (SOM⁺) inhibition is a documented control axis; according to PubMed, Niethard et al., 2017 link the PV⁺/SOM⁺ balance to state-dependent regulation of cortical circuits (DOI). (high for classes; medium for precise functional split)

II.3 Glia (non-neuronal; indispensable to dynamics)

  • Astrocytes — regulate the extracellular ionic/neurotransmitter milieu (K⁺ buffering, glutamate uptake), form the BBB's end-feet, supply metabolic support, and participate in the tripartite synapse (modulating transmission). ⟨fractal-critical: homeostatic buffering / substrate provisioning⟩ (high)
  • Oligodendrocytes — myelinate CNS axons (one cell myelinates many axons). (high)
  • Schwann cells — myelinate PNS axons (one cell, one segment); support regeneration. (high)
  • Microglia — resident immune cells; surveil, prune synapses (developmentally and in disease), respond to injury; central to neuroinflammation. ⟨fractal-critical: surveillance + pruning = structural editing⟩ (high)
  • Ependymal cells — line ventricles, produce/circulate CSF (choroid plexus). (high)
  • Satellite glia — support PNS ganglionic neurons. (high)

PART III — ELECTRICAL & CHEMICAL SIGNALING

III.1 Resting membrane potential

Neurons hold a resting potential near −65 to −70 mV (inside negative), set by ion gradients (high intracellular K⁺, high extracellular Na⁺/Cl⁻/Ca²⁺), selective permeability, and the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump (3 Na⁺ out / 2 K⁺ in). The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation predicts the potential from all permeant ions; the Nernst equation gives the equilibrium potential for one. (high, consensus)

III.2 Graded potentials

Local, decrementing voltage changes from synaptic or receptor input. Depolarizing (toward threshold) or hyperpolarizing (away). They sum spatially and temporally at the axon initial segment — the integration step. (high)

III.3 The action potential (AP)

All-or-none regenerative spike once threshold (~−55 mV) is crossed: 1. Rising phase — voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ influx, rapid depolarization to ~+30–40 mV. 2. Falling phase — Na⁺ channels inactivate; voltage-gated K⁺ channels open, K⁺ efflux, repolarization. 3. Afterhyperpolarization — brief undershoot before return to rest. - Absolute refractory period (~1–2 ms) — no second AP possible (Na⁺ channels inactivated); enforces one-way propagation and caps maximum firing rate. - Relative refractory period — a stronger-than-normal stimulus can fire. - Duration ~1 ms for the spike itself. (high, consensus) ⟨fractal-critical: refractoriness = a built-in rate limiter / dead-time after action⟩

III.4 Conduction

  • Continuous in unmyelinated axons (slow).
  • Saltatory in myelinated axons — the AP "jumps" node-to-node, fast and energy-efficient.
  • Conduction velocities range from ~0.5–2 m/s (unmyelinated C fibers) to ~80–120 m/s (large myelinated Aα fibers). Velocity scales with diameter and myelination. (high, consensus)

III.5 The synapse

  • Chemical synapse (dominant): AP → voltage-gated Ca²⁺ influx at terminal → vesicle fusion → neurotransmitter release into cleft → binds postsynaptic receptors → postsynaptic potential. Introduces a synaptic delay of ~0.5–1 ms and, crucially, directionality, gain control, plasticity, and sign (excitatory/inhibitory). (high, consensus)
  • Electrical synapse (gap junction): direct ionic coupling; near-instantaneous, bidirectional; synchronizes neuron ensembles (e.g., interneuron networks). ⟨fractal-critical: fast synchronization channel⟩ (high)

III.6 Postsynaptic integration

  • EPSP (excitatory postsynaptic potential) — depolarizing; IPSP (inhibitory) — hyperpolarizing/shunting.
  • Spatial summation (many inputs at once) and temporal summation (rapid succession) determine whether threshold is reached.
  • Dendritic computation — dendrites are not passive; NMDA spikes, Ca²⁺ spikes, and back-propagating APs let single neurons perform nonlinear, compartmentalized operations. Prediction-error-like computation may occur within dendritic compartments rather than in dedicated cells; according to PubMed, Mikulasch et al., 2023 argue for dendritic error computation in hierarchical predictive coding (DOI). (medium — active theory) ⟨fractal-critical: local error computation⟩

III.7 The neuromuscular junction (NMJ)

Specialized cholinergic synapse: motor neuron releases acetylcholine → binds nicotinic receptors on muscle → end-plate potential → muscle AP → contraction. Acetylcholinesterase terminates the signal. A model of reliable 1:1 excitatory transmission. (high)

PART IV — NEUROTRANSMITTERS & RECEPTOR SYSTEMS

IV.1 Two receptor logics

  • Ionotropic (ligand-gated ion channels) — fast (ms), direct; carry the moment-to-moment signal.
  • Metabotropic (G-protein-coupled) — slow (100s of ms–s), modulatory; change gain, excitability, and plasticity. ⟨fractal-critical: fast signal vs slow modulation is a recurring two-timescale design⟩ (high)

IV.2 Amino-acid transmitters (the workhorses)

  • Glutamate — principal excitatory transmitter. Receptors: AMPA (fast EPSP), NMDA (coincidence detector — requires both glutamate binding and postsynaptic depolarization to relieve Mg²⁺ block; gates Ca²⁺ → plasticity), kainate, and metabotropic mGluRs. ⟨fractal-critical: NMDA = AND-gate / coincidence detector driving learning⟩ (high)
  • GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid) — principal inhibitory transmitter in brain. GABA_A (ionotropic, Cl⁻, fast IPSP), GABA_B (metabotropic, slow). The brake system. ⟨fractal-critical: inhibition is load-bearing — regulation, gain control, preventing runaway⟩ (high)
  • Glycine — principal inhibitory transmitter in spinal cord/brainstem (ionotropic, Cl⁻). (high)

IV.3 Acetylcholine (ACh)

NMJ transmitter and a major CNS neuromodulator (attention, arousal, learning; basal forebrain and brainstem nuclei). Receptors: nicotinic (ionotropic) and muscarinic (metabotropic, M1–M5). Also the ANS's principal transmitter at all preganglionic synapses and parasympathetic effectors. (high)

IV.4 Monoamines (neuromodulators — set global state)

  • Dopamine (DA) — reward-prediction-error, motivation, motor control, executive function. Nuclei: substantia nigra pars compacta (→ nigrostriatal, motor), VTA (→ mesolimbic/mesocortical, reward/cognition). Receptors D1-like (D1,D5, excitatory) and D2-like (D2,D3,D4, inhibitory). DA gates plasticity (widens STDP windows). ⟨fractal-critical: teaching/reward signal that reweights the system⟩ (high)
  • Norepinephrine (NE) — arousal, vigilance, gain/"neural SNR." Source: locus coeruleus. (high)
  • Serotonin (5-HT) — mood, patience, satiety, sensory gain; source: raphe nuclei; ≥14 receptor subtypes. (high)
  • Histamine — wakefulness; tuberomammillary nucleus. (high)

IV.5 Neuropeptides & others

  • Neuropeptides (opioids/enkephalins/endorphins, substance P for pain, CRH, oxytocin, vasopressin, orexin/hypocretin for arousal, NPY for appetite) — co-released, slow, modulatory. (high)
  • Endocannabinoids (anandamide, 2-AG) — retrograde messengers: made postsynaptically, act on presynaptic CB1 to dial back input. A negative-feedback/"volume knob." ⟨fractal-critical: retrograde negative feedback⟩ According to PubMed, endocannabinoid signaling mediates glucocorticoid negative feedback on the HPA axis (Hill & Tasker, 2012, DOI). (high for retrograde role; medium for full HPA account)
  • Nitric oxide (NO) — gasotransmitter; diffusible, retrograde, vasoactive. (high)
  • ATP / adenosine (purinergic) — fast (P2X) and modulatory (adenosine → sleep pressure). (high)

IV.6 Signal termination

Reuptake (transporters: DAT, SERT, NET, EAATs for glutamate), enzymatic degradation (acetylcholinesterase, MAO, COMT), and diffusion. Termination is as important as release — it sets signal duration and prevents excitotoxic accumulation. ⟨fractal-critical: clearance/termination bounds signal persistence⟩ (high)

PART V — NEUROANATOMY: STRUCTURES AND WHAT THEY DO

V.1 Cerebral cortex

  • Lobes: Frontal (motor, executive function, planning, working memory, personality, Broca's speech). Parietal (somatosensation, spatial attention, integration). Temporal (audition, language comprehension/Wernicke's, memory via hippocampus, object recognition). Occipital (vision). Insula (interoception, taste, salience). Limbic/cingulate (emotion, motivation). (high)
  • Cortical layers (I–VI): a six-layer sheet. Layer IV = primary thalamic input (sensory); layers II/III = cortico-cortical association output; layer V = subcortical output (e.g., corticospinal); layer VI = corticothalamic feedback. This lamination implements the feedforward (up) / feedback (down) hierarchy. ⟨fractal-critical: layered hierarchy with directional in/out streams⟩ (high)
  • Cortical columns / canonical microcircuit — repeating vertical modules with a stereotyped internal wiring, hypothesized as a reusable computational unit; the predictive-coding interpretation maps forward = prediction error, backward = prediction (Shipp, 2016, DOI). (medium — canonical-circuit concept is standard; exact computation debated)
  • Primary vs association areas: primary areas (M1, S1, V1, A1) do first-order processing; association areas integrate across modalities and time. (high)

V.2 Subcortical structures

  • Basal ganglia (striatum = caudate + putamen, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, substantia nigra) — action selection, gating of motor/cognitive programs via direct (go), indirect (no-go), and hyperdirect (stop) pathways. ⟨fractal-critical: selection by disinhibition; competing go/no-go loops⟩ (high)
  • Thalamus — the great relay and gate: nearly all sensory input (except smell) synapses here en route to cortex, under strong corticothalamic feedback. Operates in tonic (faithful relay) vs burst (detection/alerting) modes. ⟨fractal-critical: central gating hub with top-down control⟩ (high)
  • Thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) — a GABAergic shell around the thalamus receiving collaterals from both cortico-thalamic and thalamo-cortical axons and inhibiting thalamic relay nuclei; implicated in sensory gating, attentional selection, and sleep spindles. According to PubMed, Ferrarelli & Tononi, 2010 review TRN's gating role (and its failure in schizophrenia) (DOI). ⟨fractal-critical: the attention searchlight / inhibitory gatekeeper⟩ (high)
  • Hypothalamus — homeostatic command center: temperature, hunger/thirst, circadian timing (SCN), autonomic and endocrine control (via pituitary). The brain's link to the body's set-points. ⟨fractal-critical: set-point regulation⟩ (high)
  • Hippocampus — declarative/episodic memory formation, spatial navigation (place cells), pattern separation/completion. (high)
  • Amygdala — threat detection, fear learning, emotional salience; note a complete fear circuit requires extinction (vmPFC-mediated) — fear acquisition without extinction is an incomplete circuit. ⟨fractal-critical: acquisition must be paired with extinction/regulation⟩ (high)
  • Basal forebrain — cholinergic source for cortical arousal/attention. (high)
  • Cerebellum — coordination, timing, motor learning, and (increasingly recognized) cognitive/temporal prediction; operates as a supervised forward-model/error-correction machine. ⟨fractal-critical: forward model + error-driven calibration⟩ (high for motor; medium for cognitive)

V.3 Brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla)

  • Midbrain — superior colliculi (visual orienting), inferior colliculi (auditory), substantia nigra, red nucleus, cerebral peduncles.
  • Pons — relay between cortex and cerebellum; sleep/arousal nuclei; several cranial-nerve nuclei.
  • Medulla oblongata — autonomic vital centers: cardiovascular (heart rate, vasomotor), respiratory rhythm, reflexes (vomiting, coughing, swallowing). Pyramidal decussation. (high)
  • Reticular formation — diffuse core network; the ascending reticular activating system (ARAS) sets arousal/consciousness level. ⟨fractal-critical: global gain/arousal control⟩ (high)

PART VI — CRANIAL NERVES (all 12: modality + innervation)

Modality key: S=sensory, M=motor, B=both, P=carries parasympathetic. (high, consensus)

  • CN I — Olfactory (S): smell. Only sense bypassing the thalamus for its primary cortex.
  • CN II — Optic (S): vision. (A CNS tract, not a true peripheral nerve.)
  • CN III — Oculomotor (M, P): most eye movements; eyelid elevation; pupil constriction + lens accommodation (parasympathetic via ciliary ganglion).
  • CN IV — Trochlear (M): superior oblique (eye down-and-in). Thinnest; only nerve exiting dorsally.
  • CN V — Trigeminal (B): facial/corneal/oral sensation (three divisions V1 ophthalmic, V2 maxillary, V3 mandibular); motor to muscles of mastication.
  • CN VI — Abducens (M): lateral rectus (eye abduction).
  • CN VII — Facial (B, P): muscles of facial expression; taste anterior ⅔ tongue; parasympathetic to lacrimal, submandibular, sublingual glands.
  • CN VIII — Vestibulocochlear (S): hearing (cochlear) + balance/head motion (vestibular).
  • CN IX — Glossopharyngeal (B, P): taste + sensation posterior ⅓ tongue; carotid baroreceptor/chemoreceptor afferents; stylopharyngeus; parotid gland (parasympathetic).
  • CN X — Vagus (B, P): the master parasympathetic nerve — innervates heart, lungs, and GI tract to the splenic flexure; visceral afferents (interoception); pharynx/larynx motor; carries the bulk of gut-brain signaling (Part IX). ⟨fractal-critical: long-range visceral feedback + parasympathetic brake⟩
  • CN XI — Accessory (M): sternocleidomastoid + trapezius (head/shoulder).
  • CN XII — Hypoglossal (M): tongue muscles.

PART VII — SPINAL CORD & SPINAL NERVES

VII.1 Organization

  • Gray matter (butterfly core): dorsal horn (sensory in), ventral horn (motor out), lateral horn (autonomic, thoracolumbar). White matter columns carry ascending/descending tracts.
  • 31 spinal nerve pairs (8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal). Dorsal root = sensory (with dorsal root ganglion), ventral root = motor — Bell–Magendie law. (high)
  • Dermatome — the skin area innervated by a single spinal segment; myotome — the muscles. Basis of clinical localization. (high)
  • Plexuses — cervical, brachial, lumbar, sacral: nerve networks redistributing spinal fibers to limbs. (high)

VII.2 Major tracts

  • Ascending (sensory): Dorsal column–medial lemniscus (DCML) — fine touch, vibration, proprioception (crosses in medulla). Spinothalamic — pain, temperature, crude touch (crosses in cord). Spinocerebellar — unconscious proprioception to cerebellum.
  • Descending (motor): Corticospinal (pyramidal) — voluntary movement. Extrapyramidal (rubrospinal, reticulospinal, vestibulospinal, tectospinal) — posture, tone, reflexive orienting. (high)

VII.3 Reflexes & pattern generators

  • Reflex arc — receptor → afferent → (interneuron) → efferent → effector; the minimal sensorimotor loop (e.g., monosynaptic stretch reflex; polysynaptic withdrawal reflex with reciprocal inhibition of antagonists). ⟨fractal-critical: local closed-loop control with built-in inhibition⟩ (high)
  • Central pattern generators (CPGs) — spinal circuits producing rhythmic output (locomotion, breathing) without ongoing input. ⟨fractal-critical: autonomous rhythmic generators⟩ (high)

PART VIII — AUTONOMIC NERVOUS SYSTEM (organ innervation)

VIII.1 The two branches (structure)

  • Sympathetic (thoracolumbar, T1–L2): short preganglionic (ACh) → sympathetic chain/prevertebral ganglia → long postganglionic (norepinephrine, mostly). Diffuse, amplifying, mobilizing.
  • Parasympathetic (craniosacral: CN III, VII, IX, X + S2–S4): long preganglionic (ACh) → ganglia near/in organ → short postganglionic (ACh). Discrete, conserving, restorative.
  • Adrenal medulla — a modified sympathetic ganglion: preganglionic fibers trigger direct release of epinephrine/norepinephrine into blood (hormonal amplification of the sympathetic signal). ⟨fractal-critical: neural signal converted to broadcast hormonal signal⟩ (high)

VIII.2 Dual innervation — organ-by-organ (sympathetic ↔ parasympathetic) (high, consensus)

  • Heart: ↑ rate & contractility (sympathetic, β1) ↔ ↓ rate (vagus, M2). Baroreflex is the negative-feedback loop stabilizing blood pressure. ⟨fractal-critical: antagonistic push–pull control⟩
  • Lungs / airways: bronchodilation (sympathetic) ↔ bronchoconstriction + secretion (vagus).
  • Pupil: dilation (sympathetic) ↔ constriction (CN III parasympathetic).
  • GI tract: ↓ motility/secretion (sympathetic) ↔ ↑ motility/secretion (vagus + sacral); local control by ENS (Part IX).
  • Salivary/lacrimal glands: scant, thick secretion (sympathetic) ↔ copious, watery (parasympathetic).
  • Bladder: relaxation + sphincter tone / storage (sympathetic) ↔ detrusor contraction / voiding (sacral parasympathetic).
  • Blood vessels: predominantly sympathetic vasoconstriction (tonic vasomotor tone); the parasympathetic role is limited.
  • Liver/adipose/pancreas: sympathetic mobilizes energy (glycogenolysis, lipolysis, ↓insulin) ↔ parasympathetic favors storage/digestion.
  • Sweat glands, piloerection, most skin vessels: sympathetic-only (note: sweat glands use ACh despite being sympathetic — an exception).
  • Reproductive organs: "point and shoot" — parasympathetic (arousal/erection) and sympathetic (emission/ejaculation) cooperate.

Principle: most viscera receive opposing inputs, and resting tone in each branch means control is bidirectional (increase or decrease from baseline), not just on/off. ⟨fractal-critical: tonic opposing drives = fine bidirectional regulation⟩

PART IX — ENTERIC NERVOUS SYSTEM & GUT–BRAIN AXIS

  • ENS structure: two plexuses in the gut wall — myenteric (Auerbach's) controls motility, submucosal (Meissner's) controls secretion/blood flow. Contains full sensory–interneuron–motor circuits and runs local reflexes autonomously. ⟨fractal-critical: a semi-autonomous peripheral "mini-brain"⟩ (high)
  • Gut–brain axis: bidirectional signaling via (1) the vagus nerve (majority afferent — the gut reports up far more than the brain commands down), (2) the immune system (cytokines), (3) endocrine signals (gut hormones, cortisol), and (4) microbial metabolites (short-chain fatty acids, neurotransmitter precursors). According to PubMed, Rutsch et al., 2020 review microbiota–gut–brain signaling and its inflammatory pathways (DOI). ⟨fractal-critical: distributed multi-channel feedback from an outside source⟩ (medium — the axis is established; specific microbe→behavior causal chains are still being worked out)
  • Serotonin note: ~90–95% of the body's serotonin is in the gut (enterochromaffin cells), acting on ENS motility and vagal afferents — an "outside source" chemically coupled to CNS state. (high)

PART X — SENSORY SYSTEMS (all senses, transduction, and pathways)

X.1 General principles (high)

  • Receptor / transduction — a specialized cell or ending converts a physical/chemical stimulus into a graded receptor potential, then spikes. Labeled lines preserve modality identity to the cortex.
  • Adaptation — tonic (slow-adapting, sustained) vs phasic (fast-adapting, change-detecting) receptors. ⟨fractal-critical: change-detection vs level-reporting⟩
  • Receptive field — the stimulus region that changes a neuron's firing; lateral inhibition sharpens contrast/edges. ⟨fractal-critical: lateral inhibition = contrast enhancement / competition⟩
  • Coding — intensity by rate + population size; quality by which lines; location by which receptors (somatotopy/retinotopy/tonotopy).

X.2 Somatosensation (touch, proprioception, temperature, pain, itch)

  • Mechanoreceptors: Merkel (fine form, SA), Meissner (flutter, FA), Pacinian (vibration, FA), Ruffini (stretch, SA). Proprioceptors: muscle spindles (length/stretch), Golgi tendon organs (tension). Thermoreceptors: warm/cold (TRP channels). Nociceptors: free endings for tissue-damaging stimuli (Aδ = fast/sharp, C = slow/burning). Pruriceptors: itch. (high)
  • Pathways: fine touch/proprioception → DCML; pain/temperature → spinothalamic. Both relay through thalamus (VPL/VPM) → S1. (high)
  • Pain is modulated, not just relayed: gate control (Aβ input inhibits nociceptive transmission) and descending modulation (periaqueductal gray → raphe → cord, opioidergic). ⟨fractal-critical: input can be gated/suppressed by parallel and top-down signals⟩ (high)

X.3 Vision

  • Retina: photoreceptors (rods = dim/monochrome; cones = bright/color, three types) → phototransduction (light → rhodopsin → closes cGMP-gated Na⁺ channels → hyperpolarization; the receptor hyperpolarizes to light — an inversion). → bipolar cells → ganglion cells (whose axons form CN II). Horizontal/amacrine cells apply lateral inhibition (center-surround). ⟨fractal-critical: center-surround = spatial band-pass filtering⟩ (high)
  • Pathway: optic nerve → optic chiasm (nasal fibers cross) → optic tract → LGN of thalamus → V1 (occipital). Parallel branch to superior colliculus (orienting) and SCN (circadian). (high)
  • Cortical processing: V1 → dorsal "where/how" (parietal) and ventral "what" (temporal) streams. (high)

X.4 Audition

  • Cochlea: sound → basilar membrane traveling wave → inner hair cells bend stereocilia → mechanotransduction (K⁺ influx) → CN VIII. Tonotopy: base = high frequency, apex = low. Outer hair cells provide active amplification/sharpening (cochlear amplifier — a local positive-feedback gain stage under efferent control). ⟨fractal-critical: active amplification with efferent gain control⟩ (high)
  • Pathway: cochlear nuclei → superior olive (binaural localization) → inferior colliculus → MGN of thalamus → A1 (temporal). (high)

X.5 Vestibular sense (balance / head motion)

  • Otolith organs (utricle, saccule) — linear acceleration/gravity. Three semicircular canals — angular/rotational acceleration (one per plane). Hair-cell transduction → CN VIII → vestibular nuclei → oculomotor (VOR — stabilizes gaze), cerebellum, cord (posture). ⟨fractal-critical: fast reflexive stabilization loop (VOR)⟩ (high)

X.6 Olfaction (smell)

  • Olfactory sensory neurons in nasal epithelium express one receptor type each; axons converge by receptor identity onto glomeruli in the olfactory bulb → olfactory cortex directly (the only sense not gated first by thalamus). Combinatorial coding across ~400 human receptor types. (high)

X.7 Gustation (taste)

  • Taste receptor cells in taste buds detect five basic modalities: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami (fat/others debated). Ionotropic (salt/sour) and GPCR (sweet/bitter/umami) transduction → CN VII, IX, X → nucleus of the solitary tract → thalamus → insula/operculum. (high)

X.8 Interoception (the body as a sensory source)

Visceral afferents (vagal + spinal) report cardiovascular, respiratory, GI, metabolic, and inflammatory state → nucleus of the solitary tract → thalamus → insula (conscious bodily feeling) + hypothalamus/brainstem (reflex control). Basis of homeostatic emotion, effort, and the "felt" body. ⟨fractal-critical: the system continuously senses its own internal state⟩ (high for pathway; medium for the affective/predictive interpretations)

PART XI — MOTOR SYSTEMS

  • Lower motor neuron (LMN) — ventral-horn neuron directly driving muscle; the "final common path." A motor unit = one LMN + all fibers it innervates. (high)
  • Upper motor neuron (UMN) — cortical/brainstem neuron commanding LMNs. Corticospinal tract = voluntary skilled movement. (high)
  • Basal ganglia loops — direct (facilitate), indirect (suppress), hyperdirect (rapid stop) select and gate actions by disinhibition of the thalamus. ⟨fractal-critical: gate-by-disinhibition; competing selection loops⟩ (high)
  • Cerebellum — forward-model prediction + error correction; coordination, timing, motor learning. ⟨fractal-critical: predictive calibration⟩ (high)
  • Descending modulators — reticulospinal (posture/tone), vestibulospinal (balance), rubrospinal, tectospinal (orienting). (high)

PART XII — NEUROENDOCRINE & BRAIN–ORGAN AXES (indirect coupling)

  • Hypothalamic–pituitary system — the neural-to-hormonal bridge. Hypothalamus → posterior pituitary (direct neural release of oxytocin, vasopressin/ADH) and → anterior pituitary (via releasing hormones controlling downstream glands). (high)
  • HPA axis (stress): CRH → ACTH → cortisol, with glucocorticoid negative feedback at pituitary, hypothalamus, and hippocampus. According to PubMed, Keller-Wood, 2015 reviews HPA feedback control (DOI); endocannabinoids mediate rapid feedback (Hill & Tasker, 2012, DOI). ⟨fractal-critical: multi-site negative feedback with fast + slow arms⟩ (high)
  • HPT axis (TRH→TSH→thyroid; metabolic set-point), HPG axis (GnRH→LH/FSH→gonads; reproduction), GH/IGF-1, prolactin — all closed-loop with negative feedback. (high)
  • Circadian system: SCN (master clock, entrained by retinal light) → pineal melatonin at night; organizes sleep, hormones, metabolism. ⟨fractal-critical: central oscillator entraining distributed clocks⟩ (high)
  • Neuro-immune coupling: microglia + cytokines + the cholinergic anti-inflammatory reflex (vagal ACh dampens peripheral inflammation) tie CNS state to immune state bidirectionally. ⟨fractal-critical: neural brake on an inflammatory positive-feedback system⟩ (medium–high)
  • Autonomic organ-control loops: baroreflex (BP), chemoreflex (O₂/CO₂ → breathing), thermoregulation, glucose/appetite (leptin, ghrelin, insulin sensed hypothalamically). Each is a sensor → controller → effector → feedback loop. ⟨fractal-critical: canonical control loops around set-points⟩ (high)

PART XIII — CIRCUIT DYNAMICS & COMPUTATIONAL PRINCIPLES

(the layer LIMEN Helix most needs — how the wiring above actually computes)

XIII.1 Excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance ⟨fractal-critical⟩

Cortical networks maintain a tight balance of excitation and inhibition; inhibition tracks excitation on a millisecond timescale. This balance sets gain, dynamic range, timing precision, and stability. Loss of inhibition → runaway excitation (seizure); excess inhibition → silence. State-dependent shifts (e.g., PV⁺ perisomatic vs SOM⁺ dendritic inhibition across sleep) tune plasticity and firing (Niethard et al., 2017, DOI). Design implication: any faithful mapping must have inhibition/"brakes" that scale with drive. (high)

XIII.2 Inhibitory motifs

  • Feedforward inhibition — input excites both a target and an interneuron that inhibits the target → enforces a narrow temporal window (coincidence-detection, "AND within a time window").
  • Feedback (recurrent) inhibition — output feeds back through interneurons → self-limiting, gain control, stability.
  • Lateral inhibition — active units suppress neighbors → contrast enhancement, winner-take-all, sparsening.
  • Disinhibition — inhibiting an inhibitor (VIP⁺→SOM⁺; basal-ganglia gating) → permissive gating / selection. ⟨fractal-critical: all four are reused at every scale; their absence breaks the fractal⟩ (high)

XIII.3 Oscillations & communication ⟨fractal-critical⟩

Rhythms create periodic windows of excitability. Canonical bands: delta (~1–4 Hz) deep sleep; theta (~4–8 Hz) memory/navigation; alpha (~8–12 Hz) idling/inhibitory gating; beta (~13–30 Hz) maintenance/motor set; gamma (~30–80+ Hz) local active processing. According to PubMed: - Communication-through-coherence: groups that oscillate coherently open input/output windows at the same time and thus communicate effectively; incoherent groups are functionally disconnected (Fries, 2005, DOI). ⟨fractal-critical: coherence = a dynamic routing/connection switch⟩ - Dynamic network communication as a balance of oscillatory coupling and local spiking, with too-strong or too-weak coupling both pathological (Voytek & Knight, 2015, DOI). ⟨fractal-critical: an optimal coupling band — extremes fail⟩ - Multi-band superposition carries distinct information simultaneously (Başar et al., 2006, DOI). - Cross-frequency coupling (e.g., theta phase organizing gamma amplitude) nests fast local processing inside slow global frames. (high for phenomena; medium for precise computational role)

XIII.4 Thalamocortical gating ⟨fractal-critical⟩

The thalamus + TRN gate what reaches cortex; tonic/burst modes and TRN inhibition implement an attentional/sensory filter under corticothalamic (top-down) control (Ferrarelli & Tononi, 2010, DOI). Design implication: gating must occur at the right stage — filter too early (before the circuit that needs the signal) and you lose information you can't recover. (high)

XIII.5 Predictive coding / prediction error ⟨fractal-critical⟩

The cortex is modeled as a hierarchical generative machine: top-down predictions descend, bottom-up prediction errors ascend, and only the mismatch propagates upward — an efficiency and learning principle. Laminar/microcircuit correlates: forward = error, backward = prediction (Shipp, 2016, DOI); errors may be computed in dendrites (Mikulasch et al., 2023, DOI). ⟨fractal-critical: "prediction error drives update" is the candidate unified rule that must also generate predictions at business scale⟩ (medium — leading framework, not universally settled)

XIII.6 Plasticity (learning rules) ⟨fractal-critical⟩

  • Hebbian ("fire together, wire together") — correlated activity strengthens synapses.
  • STDP (spike-timing-dependent plasticity) — order and timing matter: pre-before-post (~+10 ms window) → LTP; post-before-pre → LTD. Neuromodulators reshape the window — dopamine extends the LTP window from ~+10 to ~+30 ms and can even convert anti-Hebbian timing to potentiation (Ruan et al., 2014, DOI). ⟨fractal-critical: a teaching signal reshapes the learning rule itself⟩
  • LTP/LTD — long-term potentiation/depression; NMDA-receptor / Ca²⁺-dependent; the cellular substrate of memory.
  • Homeostatic plasticity / synaptic scaling — global normalization that keeps overall drive in range so Hebbian learning doesn't run away. ⟨fractal-critical: a stabilizing counter-process to positive-feedback learning⟩
  • Metaplasticity — plasticity of plasticity; the threshold for change itself adapts. (high for existence; medium for exact in-vivo parameters)

XIII.7 Neuromodulation & gain ⟨fractal-critical⟩

Diffuse systems (DA, NE, 5-HT, ACh) don't carry detailed content; they set global regime — gain, signal-to-noise, exploration/exploitation, learning rate, arousal. A small modulatory signal reconfigures the whole network's operating point. (high)

XIII.8 Population & attractor dynamics

Information lives in distributed population activity, not single cells; sparse coding (few active units) is efficient and separable; attractor dynamics implement stable states (memories, decisions) and transitions. Emergence arises from many simple units under E/I balance — bottlenecking any of these (over-gating, over-synchrony, lost inhibition) suppresses the emergence. ⟨fractal-critical: emergence requires distributed, sparse, inhibition-balanced activity⟩ (medium–high)

XIII.9 Sleep, homeostasis & offline processing

Sleep down-scales synapses globally while consolidating select memories (Niethard et al., 2017, DOI) and drives glymphatic clearance (Benveniste et al., 2018, DOI). ⟨fractal-critical: mandatory offline maintenance/reset phase — a system with no "downtime" accumulates damage⟩ (medium–high)

PART XIV — FAILURE MODES / PATHOLOGY AT THE NEURAL SCALE

(Included deliberately: a complete map includes how each mechanism breaks. Each failure is the pathological form of a dynamic above.)

  • Seizure / epilepsy — E/I balance failure: runaway recurrent excitation when inhibition (GABAergic braking) is insufficient. The archetypal "no brakes → runaway" pathology. ⟨fractal-critical: loss of load-bearing inhibition⟩ (high)
  • Excitotoxicity — excessive glutamate/NMDA → Ca²⁺ overload → cell death; failure of termination/clearance. ⟨fractal-critical: signal that isn't cleared becomes toxic⟩ (high)
  • Cortical spreading depression — a slow wave of depolarization then silence; homeostatic overwhelm (migraine aura). (high)
  • Sensory-gating failure — TRN/thalamocortical gating breakdown (e.g., schizophrenia: reduced sleep spindles, impaired gating; Ferrarelli & Tononi, 2010, DOI); too little filtering → flooding, or mis-timed gating → wrong information passed. ⟨fractal-critical: gating failure = signal overload or starvation⟩ (medium–high)
  • Disinhibition syndromes — losing the inhibitor releases pathological activity (basal-ganglia disorders; loss of prefrontal control). (high)
  • Oscillatory pathology — over-coupling (Parkinsonian beta hypersynchrony) or under-coupling; both degrade communication (Voytek & Knight, 2015, DOI). ⟨fractal-critical: extremes of coupling both fail⟩ (medium–high)
  • Neurodegeneration — protein aggregation (amyloid-β, tau, α-synuclein) + failed clearance (glymphatic decline with age; Benveniste et al., 2018, DOI) + network collapse. ⟨fractal-critical: maintenance failure compounding over time⟩ (medium–high)
  • Deafferentation / diaschisis — losing input (stroke, lesion) disables not just the lesioned node but distant connected regions; the network, not the node, is the unit of failure. ⟨fractal-critical: failures propagate across the graph⟩ (high)
  • Incomplete-circuit pathology — a circuit built without its regulatory partner is intrinsically dysfunctional (fear acquisition without extinction; excitation without matched inhibition; feedback without the sensor). ⟨fractal-critical: this is the direct diagnostic for a broken fractal mapping — if the business/civilization mapping omits the inhibitory/feedback/extinction arm, it is lesioned in the same way a brain is⟩ (high, conceptual)
